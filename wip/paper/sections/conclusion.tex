\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a continuous collision detection pipeline that is conservative
by construction on two very different processors, and measured it against a
certified reference on a dataset with exact roots. On the host, the median and
worst-case error match TightInclusion's on all twelve scene-phases to every
printed digit at between $2.1\times$ and $3.5\times$ its speed, so the pipeline
reproduces the reference exactly; on the device, faster again by $2.6\times$ to
$20.9\times$, the two agree to within a few percent.

The design result generalises beyond this problem. A branch-and-bound search
becomes order-independent once it keeps a minimum over all accepted leaves, in
place of returning the first leaf a priority queue supplies
(\cref{prop:order}), and order-independence then allows vector lanes and thread
blocks to be filled with work from unrelated queries. Both of our parallel
kernels are consequences of that observation, applied in opposite directions:
packing lanes across queries on the host, and pushing work out of the block that
generated it on the device.

Two results are negative. Cheap geometric statistics computed from the input
fail to predict which broad phase will be faster, so the choice is settled by
measurement, and the cell list leads the sweep on every scene and both
processors. And a principled splitter aimed at the largest root-free region
loses to a crude clamped one, because a branch-and-bound search can exploit only
volume that a \emph{single} component rules out, and a multi-way split allows
different children to die by different components.

Three extensions follow directly: minimum-separation queries, which the
barrier-method solvers this work targets generally require; multi-GPU execution,
which suits the double-buffered queue; and an in-solver evaluation, the most
important of the three, without which the practical value of these speedups
remains inferred and unmeasured.
