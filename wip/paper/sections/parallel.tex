\section{Parallel implementation}
\label{sec:parallel}

\Cref{prop:order} establishes that the search may be explored in any order. The
two narrow-phase implementations use that freedom in opposite directions: on the
host, vector lanes are filled with work drawn from unrelated queries
(\cref{alg:host}); on the device, work is moved \emph{out} of the block that
generated it (\cref{alg:device}). The broad phase needs no equivalent treatment,
since its parallelism is already the outermost loop of
\cref{alg:cell,alg:cellmin} and its count-then-fill output carries no
contention. Both narrow-phase designs respond to the same property of the
problem.

The cost of a CCD query is heavy-tailed. \Cref{tab:difficulty} counts, for one
scene, how many boxes each query needed. Most queries are decided by a single
box and a small number need millions. Any parallel design that pins a query to
a fixed execution resource is therefore load-imbalanced by construction, and the
imbalance is not a factor of two.

The design responds to the host column. The device column sets the size of the
tail a work list has to survive, the subject of \cref{sec:parallel:device}; it
predates the redesign it motivated and it aggregates two code paths, so we draw
no host-versus-device conclusion from it.
\Cref{sec:results:proc} makes that comparison under controlled conditions.

\subsection{Host: packing lanes across queries}
\label{sec:parallel:host}

\begin{algorithm}[!tb]
\caption{Host narrow phase. Lanes hold boxes from different queries, which
\cref{prop:order} permits. $V$ is the vector width and $D$ the depth cap.}
\label{alg:host}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{HostNarrowPhase}{candidates $\Cand$}
  \ForAll{blocks $Q$ of $V$ consecutive queries \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State load geometry, $\tau$, $\epsilon$ per query;\;
           $\toi_{q} \gets$ shared minimum
    \State $S \gets$ stack holding the root box of each query of $Q$; all $V$
           lanes idle
    \While{true}
      \ForAll{idle lanes $l$}
        \State pop $\dom$ from $S$, discarding any with
               $\dom.t_{\ell} \ge \toi_{\dom.q}$
          \Comment{refill}
        \State bind $\dom$ to lane $l$, reloading lane geometry only if
               $\dom.q$ changed
      \EndFor
      \State \textbf{if} no lane is live \textbf{then break}
      \State $[\underline{F},\overline{F}] \gets$ hull of the $8$ stored
             corners, per component, all lanes at once
      \ForAll{live lanes $l$}
        \Comment{phase 1: classify, evaluating nothing}
        \If{$\dom_{l}$ is rejected by the test of \cref{eq:err}}
          \State lane $l \gets$ idle
        \ElsIf{\Call{Accept}{$\dom_{l}$} \textbf{or} $\mathrm{depth}_{l} \ge D$}
          \State $\toi_{q_{l}} \gets \min(\toi_{q_{l}},\ \dom_{l}.t_{\ell})$;\;
                 lane $l \gets$ idle
        \Else
          \State $a_{l} \gets \arg\max_{k} \dom_{l}.w_{k}/\tau_{k}$;\;
                 $m_{l} \gets$ midpoint of axis $a_{l}$
          \State \textbf{if} $m_{l}$ does not separate the endpoints
                 \textbf{then} accept and idle lane $l$
          \State \textbf{else} mark $l$ as splitting
        \EndIf
      \EndFor
      \State lay out the $4$ mid-face corner parameters of every splitting lane
      \State evaluate $\Fdiff$ there in \emph{one} vector sweep across all lanes
        \Comment{phase 2}
      \ForAll{splitting lanes $l$}
        \Comment{phase 3: assemble}
        \State push the upper child: the parent's outer face, the new mid face
        \State keep the lower child in lane $l$, overwritten in place
          \Comment{depth-first in $t$}
      \EndFor
    \EndWhile
    \State write $\toi_{q}$ out and lower the shared minimum by compare-and-swap
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

By \cref{tab:difficulty}, a per-query SIMD kernel would spend most of its lanes
idle. The \textsc{Tight} host kernel of \cref{alg:host} therefore treats the
\emph{box} as the unit of work and keeps a vector of $V$ lanes ($V=8$ on
AVX-512 and AVX2, $4$ on NEON). Each lane holds a box that may belong to a
different query, and a lane whose box is decided is refilled from a shared
stack, reloading that lane's geometry only when the query changes.
\Cref{prop:order} makes this legal: since the answer does not depend on the
order in which the tree is walked, lanes need not agree about which query they
are working on.

Two design decisions follow from that and carry most of the performance.

\emph{Each box stores the value of $\Fdiff$ at its eight corners.} A bisection
splits a box at a mid-face, so the two children share that face and each
inherits its outer face from the parent. A split therefore evaluates four
mid-face corners, where a split without inheritance evaluates eight for each of
two children, a fourfold reduction in the operation that dominates the kernel.
The cost is $3\times8$ scalars carried per live box, which is cheap relative to
what it saves.

\emph{Geometry is replicated per lane.} Because lanes hold different queries,
indexing the geometry by a lane's query would make every corner evaluation a
gather. Copying the $30$ values a lane needs when it is refilled turns the inner
loop into contiguous loads, and a refill is far cheaper than the $24$ corner
evaluations it feeds.

The iteration then has three phases. \emph{Classify} decides every lane's fate
from bounds the lane already carries and evaluates nothing. \emph{Evaluate}
lays out the mid-face corner parameters of every lane and performs one flat
vector sweep, even though different lanes are splitting different axes; lanes
that are not splitting are predicated out, and since about half the lanes split
on a given iteration this removes roughly a quarter of the kernel's total work.
\emph{Assemble} pushes the upper child to the stack and keeps the \emph{lower}
child in the lane. Each lane is therefore depth-first in $t$, so the running
minimum converges sooner and prunes harder, and the arrangement also halves the
stack traffic and skips a refill.

The two query types peak at different lane widths. A vertex-face corner costs
one more multiply-add and one more subtraction than an edge-edge corner, so its
vector sweeps take longer to amortise the per-lane scalar work between them, and
it peaks narrower. On NEON, over the whole scene, narrowing vertex-face from
four lanes to two moves it from $297.7$ to $246.0\,\mathrm{ms}$, and widening
edge-edge from two to four moves it from $599.9$ to $494.6\,\mathrm{ms}$, while
eight and sixteen lanes are worse for both. The x86 widths quoted above follow
from the same operation count and have not been measured.

A measurement caveat attaches to those figures and generalises beyond this
kernel: the two query types must be timed in separate processes. Timed
together, the edge-edge figure varies by about $20\%$ with the lane width chosen
for the \emph{vertex-face} instantiation, which the measurement never executes.
The effect is one of code layout and not of work, and it is large enough to
manufacture or conceal a difference of the size reported here.

\subsection{Device: a small stack, and a queue that redistributes}
\label{sec:parallel:device}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \input{tikz/gpu-workflow}
  \caption{The device narrow phase. Each block keeps a small stack in shared
    memory and spills the rest into one half of a double-buffered global queue;
    a launch writes one half and reads the other, so a producer and its consumer
    always sit in different launches. At the buffer swap the subtree of a
    pathological query becomes available to every block, where a large shared
    stack would have kept it inside the block that seeded it.}
  \label{fig:gpu-workflow}
\end{figure}

\begin{algorithm}[!tb]
\caption{Device narrow phase: a kernel that runs to completion on the work it
was given, and a host loop that relaunches it on what it spilled. $S$ is the per-block shared stack ($64$ entries) and $G$ the
double-buffered global queue.}
\label{alg:device}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Kernel}{read buffer $G_{\mathrm{in}}$, write buffer $G_{\mathrm{out}}$}
  \State thread $t$ takes one box: its own query's root, or one entry of
         $G_{\mathrm{in}}$
  \While{any thread of the block is live \textbf{or} $S \ne \emptyset$}
    \State refresh the block's bound from the global minimum
      \Comment{every iteration; see text}
    \State \textbf{if} $\dom.t_{\ell} \ge$ bound \textbf{then} idle this thread
    \If{$\mathrm{depth} \ge D$ \textbf{or} $\dom$ cannot be split}
      \State lower $\toi$ to $\dom.t_{\ell}$; idle this thread
        \Comment{exhaustion accepts}
    \Else
      \State $(\dom^{-},\dom^{+}) \gets$ \Call{Split}{$\dom$}
      \State evaluate \textbf{all $8$ corners of each child}
        \Comment{all eight; \cref{alg:host} inherits four}
      \State keep those that contain the origin and can still improve the bound
      \State \textbf{if} both survive \textbf{then} descend into the smaller
             $t_{\ell}$, set the other aside
      \State \textbf{else if} one survives \textbf{then} descend into it
             \textbf{else} idle this thread
    \EndIf
    \If{a box is set aside}
      \State claim a slot in $S$; if $S$ is full, bump-allocate in
             $G_{\mathrm{out}}$
      \State if $G_{\mathrm{out}}$ is full, count the deficit and \textbf{drop}
        \Comment{safe, \cref{sec:guarantee}}
    \EndIf
    \State idle threads claim from $S$ by compare-and-swap, reloading on a
           query change
  \EndWhile
\EndFunction
\Statex
\Function{Host}{candidates $\Cand$}
  \State \Call{Kernel}{seeds, $A$};\quad $r \gets 0$
  \While{the buffer just written is non-empty \textbf{and} $r < 32$}
    \State \Call{Kernel}{it, the other one}; swap; $r \gets r+1$
      \Comment{reader and writer never meet}
  \EndWhile
  \State \textbf{if} the deficit is non-zero \textbf{then} grow $G$ and rerun,
         keeping the times of impact
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The device kernel runs one thread per query. \Cref{fig:gpu-workflow} gives its
shape and \cref{alg:device} gives it in full. Three decisions determine its
behaviour, and in each of them the straightforward alternative fails.

\paragraph{The global queue.} The queue is double-buffered: a launch reads one
buffer and
writes the other, never both, so there is no producer--consumer handshake and
nothing spins. A single shared buffer, in which writers spin on a
compare-and-swap until a reader releases the slot, is invisible while the queue
is lightly used and \emph{hangs the kernel} once the queue is driven hard, the
condition created by using it to balance load. Separating the buffers removes
the interaction outright.

\paragraph{The shared stack.} The shared stack is deliberately small. The
per-block stack is where
a query's subtree lives, so a large one keeps a heavy query inside the block
that seeded it. By \cref{tab:difficulty}, one block can work through a
$19.7$-million-box query while its neighbours finish and idle. Spilling early
hands those boxes to the global queue, and the next launch spreads them over
every block. \Cref{tab:stackcap} measures the effect.

\paragraph{Overflow handling.} A box that does not fit in the
global queue is counted and discarded, which is safe since dropping a box can
only leave the reported time of impact earlier than the truth, never later
(\cref{sec:guarantee}). The host reads the deficit, grows the queue and re-runs
the batch, keeping the times of impact found so far so that later attempts prune
harder. The retry is bounded at $32$ rounds, since giving up keeps the result
conservative whereas spinning does not keep it finite.

Two smaller decisions were also settled by measurement. The first is how often
the running minimum is re-read from global memory, and it is re-read on
\emph{every} iteration. Refreshing every 4th, 16th, 64th or 256th iteration was
monotonically worse on all three scenes tested, a whole-scene $638 \to
3377\,\mathrm{ms}$ on cloth-funnel at 256, because the pruning a fresh bound
buys exceeds what the atomic costs. The second is the unit of work. One thread
per query beat one block per query by $4.47\times$, $2.76\times$ and
$4.43\times$ on the three scenes, because the block-per-query kernel was bound
by \emph{scheduling blocks} and not by the search: $843{,}414$ blocks each
classifying $63$ boxes across $128$ threads is half a box per thread.

The device broad phase is a more conventional port: count, prefix sum and
scatter, with a radix sort and a device scan from CUB~\citep{cub} replacing
their host equivalents, one box per thread, and a scalar inner loop where the
host uses its $32$-lane mask. Its shape suits a GPU, since no part of it walks
a window sequentially.

\subsection{Precision}
\label{sec:parallel:precision}

Both narrow phases compute in double precision internally, regardless of the
type the caller stores geometry in, and this is a requirement of the method.
With single-precision arithmetic the certified bound of \cref{eq:err} and the
tolerances of \cref{eq:tol} sit too close together for the search to retain its
guarantee. The single-precision device kernel reported nine times of impact
\emph{later} than the true root on armadillo-rollers, which violates
\cref{def:conservative}.

The interface remains templated on the storage type, so a caller with float
geometry keeps float buffers. Widening on load is exact and needs no care,
whereas narrowing the computed time of impact back to float does.
Round-to-nearest can round \emph{up}, publishing a value later than the one the
search proved, so the narrowing rounds toward $-\infty$. Reporting earlier is
always safe, and reporting later is the failure the whole design exists to
prevent.

\subsection{Why the work divides the way it does}
\label{sec:parallel:why}

\Cref{tab:difficulty} shows the device exploring many more boxes than the host on
the same queries, and \cref{sec:results:proc} shows the device narrow phase
losing to the host on four of six scenes. Both have the same two structural
causes, and neither is a property of the hardware.

First, the device re-evaluates all eight corners of both children at every
split, where the host inherits four of them from the parent
(\cref{sec:parallel:host}). That factor applies to the dominant operation and is
paid on every box.

Second, the bound a box is pruned against is probably sharper on the host at the
moment it is needed; this is a candidate explanation and not an established one.
Host lanes descend depth-first in $t$ and publish continuously, so a query
benefits from its neighbours' progress almost immediately, whereas the device
walks a far wider frontier at any instant. The alternatives are easier to rule
out than this explanation is to confirm. The seeding is not responsible, since
it would show on the easy queries too. Neither the acceptance test, the
tolerances nor the split rule can be responsible either, since all three are
transcribed from the host and would shift the whole distribution uniformly,
whereas the measurement shows a stretched tail. Establishing the remaining
explanation requires a single hard query traced box by box, which we have not
done.

The broad phase inverts both arguments, since it has no sequential dependence
and no shared bound to lose. The GPU accordingly wins that phase on all six
scenes, and the pipeline wins end to end on four of them despite losing the
narrow phase on four. \Cref{sec:results} measures all of it.

\subsection{Where the two processors spend their time}
\label{sec:parallel:limits}

The previous subsection explains the device's disadvantage in structural terms,
and hardware counters identify the resource each processor is short of.
\Cref{tab:counters} is Nsight Compute over the device search kernel and
\cref{tab:counters-host,tab:symbols} are \texttt{perf} on the host, six scenes
each, at the \op{Tight} mode and the shipped parameters.

\paragraph{Memory traffic.} The device is not short of bandwidth. Across the
six scenes the search moves between $0.8$ and $220\,\mathrm{MB}$ of dynamic
random-access memory (DRAM) traffic per launch, and never exceeds $1.2\%$ of
sustained DRAM peak. Arithmetic needs more care, because the natural measure of
it is the wrong one here. \Cref{tab:pipes} reports what the units are doing, and
\cref{tab:throughput} reports the throughput that matters here, candidate pairs
resolved per second. The cache hierarchy explains the quiet memory system. On
armadillo-rollers the kernel touches $63.9\,\mathrm{MB}$ of L1 sectors,
$20.3\,\mathrm{MB}$ of those reach L2, and only $2.3\,\mathrm{MB}$ reach DRAM. A
box is small, and its parent's corners are still resident when its children are
built.

\paragraph{Functional-unit utilisation.} The functional units are not idle; the
multiprocessor is. Two columns of \cref{tab:pipes} are normalised differently.
The pipe columns give each unit's share of its peak issue rate \emph{while the
multiprocessor is active}, and on that basis the double-precision pipe runs at
$18$ to $29\%$ of peak and the integer arithmetic logic unit (ALU) at $15$ to
$25\%$, close behind it on every scene. The \emph{SM} column is the whole
multiprocessor's share of peak over \emph{elapsed} time, and on the smallest
scene it is $2.25\%$. Pipes that issue at a quarter of peak when they have warps
to issue from do not limit this kernel, and cheaper arithmetic would not move
it.

The elapsed-time column also shows where the grid stops mattering. It climbs with
the launch size, from $2.25\%$ at $0.41$ waves per multiprocessor to $28.4\%$ at
$4.44$, and then stops: n-body and puffer-ball, at $333$ and $475$ waves, reach
$26.4\%$ and $28.6\%$. Four waves is enough to cover the device, and the hundred
after that buy nothing.

No flop rate is reported here. A rate against arithmetic peak is the figure of
merit for a dense kernel, whose work is fixed and whose only question is how
fast it can be pushed through, whereas the work of a branch-and-bound search is
not fixed and the whole design exists to do less of it. Corner inheritance in
\cref{alg:host} turns eight corner evaluations into four, and sharing a bound
across queries takes the search from about eleven boxes per query to roughly
one. Both cut the flop count, and both make the implementation better. An
implementation that raised its flop rate while returning the same answers would
be a worse one. The rate therefore carries no information about quality, and the
comparison against peak none about headroom. What we claim from the pipe columns
is narrower: no functional unit is saturated, so no functional unit is the
limit.

The instruction mix settles one more question, the kind of arithmetic the search
does. Integer thread-instructions run at $0.58$ to $0.95$ per double-precision
instruction, so the kernel does nearly as much integer work as floating-point
work. That integer work is the algorithm itself: stack indices, query
identifiers, depth counters, the barycentric validity test and the atomics of
\cref{alg:device}. A corner evaluation is double-precision work, and a box is
more than a corner evaluation. The load-store pipe never exceeds $8.1\%$, the
instruction-side counterpart of the quiet DRAM above.

\paragraph{Available parallelism.} The kernel is not short of work to schedule
either, and six scenes establish this where two would not. The grid size spans
three orders of magnitude, from $0.41$ waves per multiprocessor on cloth-funnel,
where the launch does not cover the device even once, to $475$ on puffer-ball,
where it covers it hundreds of times over. Occupancy moves hardly at all across
that range, from $8.4\%$ to $16.3\%$, and the issue rate stays between $0.20$
and $0.35$ instructions per scheduler cycle out of a possible one. A kernel
short of parallelism would improve when handed four hundred times as much of it,
and this one does not.

\paragraph{Latency.} The limit is latency, and the same latency on every scene.
The last three columns of \cref{tab:counters} report the stall terms. Warps
stalled on an execution dependency sit between $1.52$ and $1.65$ on every scene,
a spread of $8\%$ across inputs whose launches differ by three orders of
magnitude in size and whose candidate pairs per step differ by a factor of seven
hundred. Stalls on memory are the smallest term almost everywhere, between
$0.17$ and $1.40$. A corner evaluation is a chain of dependent multiply--adds,
and with $8$ to $16\%$ of the warp slots filled, and $8$ to $24$ of a warp's
$32$ lanes doing useful work when an instruction issues, there are not enough
independent warps resident to cover that chain. Adding grid does not add
residency.

Barrier stalls are the one term that does move, from $0.99$ to $2.98$, with
puffer-ball at the top of that range. Three block-wide barriers per iteration
cost what the slowest thread in the block costs, and puffer-ball has both the
most candidate pairs per step and the widest spread of query difficulty. The
stall does not decide the outcome: puffer-ball's device narrow phase leads the
host by $15.80\times$ in \cref{tab:processor}, the largest such margin in the
benchmark. The counter bounds how much of that margin the barriers cost, and not
whether the device wins.

\paragraph{Host counters.} Grace is the opposite case. The host pipeline retires
between $2.8$ and $3.8$ instructions per cycle at an L1 data miss rate of $0.2$
to $2.1\%$, and the narrow phase on its own misses L1 on between $0.01$ and
$0.65\%$ of loads. The per-lane geometry replication of \cref{sec:parallel:host}
turns a gather into contiguous loads, and the counters show it doing so: a
lane's working set stays resident. Its instruction rate tracks how hard the
queries are and not how many there are. It is $0.49$ on cloth-ball, whose
searches are deep, against $2.87$ on n-body, where the median answer is within
$10^{-8}$ of the root and most queries are settled by a single box. That is the
same dependent chain the device stalls on, paid on a core with enough
out-of-order capacity to hide most of it.

\paragraph{Host costs outside the timings.} \Cref{tab:symbols} attributes
sampled host cycles to the object that spent them. On cloth-funnel $14.1\%$ of
them are inside \texttt{libm}, and the symbol profile puts all of that in one
call, the \texttt{pow} that raises $\delta$ to the third power in
\cref{eq:err}. The share is scene-dependent, falling to $2.6\%$ on
armadillo-rollers. It is written as \texttt{std::pow} deliberately.
\Cref{sec:prelim:err} records that the error bound is certified for one sequence
of operations, and the host keeps the reference's sequence so that its answers
stay bit-identical to it (\cref{sec:results:ref}). The device kernel does not
pay this cost, since it computes $\delta^{3}$ as a plain cube scaled to round
upward. The host could do the same, at the price of the exact agreement that
\cref{sec:results:ref} reports.

The OpenMP runtime is the second such cost, and at its worst it is the largest
of the three: $26.9\%$ of cycles on armadillo-rollers against $0.3\%$ on
puffer-ball. The ordering follows case count and not problem size.
Armadillo-rollers runs $781$ cases and puffer-ball $240$, and each case enters
and leaves the parallel regions of \cref{alg:cell,alg:cellmin} and the narrow
phase afresh. A scene made of many small steps therefore pays the runtime
repeatedly, whereas a scene made of few large ones amortises it away. SCCD's own
code accounts for the rest, between $45$ and $90\%$ of sampled cycles, depending
on how much of the profile the runtime and the operating system take.

\paragraph{Scope of the counters.} These counters cover one narrow-phase mode
and the earliest-impact kernel. The device figures are taken under a profiler
that serialises launches, so they support statements about ratios and none about
absolute time. The host rows are whole-process counters and not per-phase ones.
The pipeline row comes from a build with the device path compiled out, so it is
host work throughout; the narrow-phase row comes from the oracle, which also
drives the device modes, so its instruction rate is a floor. One prediction is
left untested, and it belongs to \cref{sec:parallel:device}: if idle threads
could claim new queries from a global cursor, the dependency and barrier stalls
should fall together. Nothing here confirms that they would.
