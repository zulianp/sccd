\section{Broad phase}
\label{sec:bp}

The broad phase answers the cheaper of the two questions, never evaluating
$\Fdiff$ and never seeing a tolerance: it turns a mesh into candidate pairs,
primitives whose swept boxes overlap and might therefore touch, and hands them to
\cref{sec:np}. It must be conservative, since a discarded pair is never examined
again, and on our benchmark scenes it is the larger share of the host runtime, so
it must be fast. One algorithm over one uniform grid on two of the three axes
serves both its queries, which differ in shape: vertex-face bins a box into every
cell its extent touches (\cref{sec:bp:cell}), while edge-edge, one list against
itself, admits a cheaper binning and a different walk (\cref{sec:bp:self}).

\subsection{Swept boxes, and the shape of the output}
\label{sec:bp:common}

Because vertices move affinely, the axis-aligned box enclosing a primitive's
whole trajectory is the componentwise hull of its vertex positions at $t=0$ and
$t=1$, exact and needing no sampling; one box is built per vertex, per edge and
per face, and where a box is stored in a narrower type than the geometry each
bound moves outward by one unit in the last place, so a rounded box can never
exclude a candidate the exact box would have kept. The overlap test rejects only
on a \emph{strict} separation, $a_{\min} > b_{\max}$ on some axis, so boxes that
merely touch are reported as overlapping --- deliberately, since a candidate
window rejecting boxes in contact drops the degenerate pairs a moving surface
produces most often. All storage is structure-of-arrays and every output is
built \emph{count-then-fill}: a first pass counts the pairs each primitive will
contribute, a prefix sum turns those counts into offsets, and a second pass
re-runs the identical traversal, writing into its own slice, so nothing grows a
shared buffer, the allocation is exact, the output is bit-for-bit reproducible,
and the same shape works on a GPU, where a growable per-thread container is not
available. The host tests one box against $32$ consecutive boxes at a time, an
AVX-512, AVX2 or NEON kernel returning a $32$-bit mask of the survivors, so
counting is a population count and enumeration a bit scan, and a rejected
candidate never issues the indirect load of its element indices, the expensive
part of a test that usually fails.

\subsection{The vertex-face query}
\label{sec:bp:cell}

Vertex-face takes two lists, the faces against the vertices, and
\cref{alg:cell} answers it by binning one of them into a grid over \emph{two} of
the three axes: for a surface of $N$ elements of size $h$ in a region of side
$L$ we have $N \sim (L/h)^{2}$, since a surface does not fill a volume, so a
two-dimensional grid at element resolution has $O(N)$ cells and is mostly
occupied, where a three-dimensional one has $(L/h)^{3} = N^{3/2}$ cells --- at
$N = 1.5\times10^{6}$, $1.8\times10^{9}$ cells at $0.08\%$ occupancy, spending
memory and empty-cell iteration to prune along a direction the surface barely
occupies. The third axis still culls, through the ordinary three-dimensional box
test inside the cell, where it costs nothing extra. The spacing on each of the
two axes is the \emph{mean extent of the swept boxes} on that axis, the
resulting cell counts scaled isotropically so that their product does not exceed
$4N$: past that point more resolution does not pay, and a degenerate extent
could otherwise allocate without bound. The caller supplies no voxel size.

Two other spacings perform worse. The \emph{element} size makes the grid far too
fine, since a swept box is much larger than its element, so every box would span
a block of cells and binning would cost more than the query saves. The
\emph{largest} box, the textbook uniform-grid rule, buys a property the mean
lacks: with no box wider than a cell, two overlapping boxes lie within one cell
of each other, so a fixed $3\times3$ stencil around one owning cell finds every
partner, with no duplicates to settle. Its cost is that the widest box in the
scene sets the spacing for all of them, and swept box sizes span a wide range
--- the widest is $4.5\times$ the mean on armadillo-rollers' vertices and
$24\times$ on cloth-funnel's edges, since a few elements move fast while most
barely move. On armadillo-rollers one swept edge spans $11\%$ of the scene, so a
cell at least that wide admits an $8\times9$ grid at any element count, and the
scene's $36{,}363$ edges land in $72$ cells, $505$ to a cell, which measured
costs the host broad phase up to $11.9\times$. The mean instead keeps a box's
cost proportional to its own size, as a heavy-tailed size distribution requires.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The vertex-face query, one pass (count or fill)}
\label{alg:cell}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{CellList}{$A$, $B$}
  \State $(a_{0},a_{1}) \gets$ the two axes of largest centre variance
  \State $h_{k} \gets$ mean extent of the boxes on axis $a_{k}$;\quad
         $n_{k} \gets \lfloor \operatorname{span}_{k}/h_{k} \rfloor$,
         scaled so $n_{0}n_{1} \le 4N$
  \State count the cells of each box of $B$, prefix-sum them, and scatter
         each box index into its cells
  \ForAll{$i$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \ForAll{cells $c$ touched by $A_{i}$, and $j$ listed in $c$}
      \If{$A_{i}$ and $B_{j}$ are disjoint in 3D} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
      \State $o_{k} \gets \max(A_{i}.\min_{a_{k}},\, B_{j}.\min_{a_{k}})$
        \Comment{corner of the overlap}
      \If{$c \ne \operatorname{cell}(o_{0},o_{1})$} \textbf{continue}
        \Comment{another cell owns this pair}
      \EndIf
      \If{$A_{i}$ and $B_{j}$ share a vertex} \textbf{continue} \EndIf
      \State count, or emit the pair
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Construction applies the count-then-fill of \cref{sec:bp:common} to cells
(\cref{fig:cell2d}(a)), both passes sharing one function for deciding which
cells a box covers, so they cannot disagree and corrupt the cell array silently
and without failing; the pair output is built the same way, so the whole broad
phase performs exactly two allocations, both of counted sizes. Duplicates are
then settled without per-pair state. A box spans several cells, so a pair can be
discovered in several of them (\cref{fig:cell2d}(b)), and the usual remedies, a
hash set of emitted pairs or a mark array, cost memory proportional to the
number of pairs and are awkward on a GPU. The pair is therefore
\emph{attributed} to the cell holding the minimum corner of the two boxes'
overlap, on line 8 of \cref{alg:cell}: that corner lies inside both boxes, so
both are binned there, and the cell is unique. Costing two clamps per surviving
pair and carrying no state, the rule runs unchanged on the device.

Nothing here is sorted and nothing assumes an order, since the query tests every
entry of a touched cell and never breaks on a first miss. Binning costs
$O\bigl(\sum_{i}\text{cells}(i)\bigr)$ and the query
$O\bigl(\sum_{i}\sum_{c \ni i}\text{occupancy}(c)\bigr)$, with no logarithmic
factor in either, where $\text{cells}(i)$ is the footprint of box $i$ at the
mean spacing, $O(1)$ in the mean and larger for the few boxes that are larger: a
box pays for its own size and for no other box's.
\Cref{sec:results:scaling} measures the exponents both achieve in practice.

\subsection{The edge-edge query}
\label{sec:bp:self}

Edge-edge is one list against itself, which admits a cheaper binning than
\cref{sec:bp:cell} uses: a box enters the \emph{single} cell holding its minimum
corner, so the cell array holds one entry per box, a partner is met at most
once, and the duplicate rule of \cref{alg:cell} becomes unnecessary. Reading
only the cells that come after a box's own is how linked-cell neighbour search
avoids handling a pair twice, each cell visiting half its
neighbours~\citep{allen2017liquids}; that walk is sound there because a particle
is a point binned by its centre in a cell at least the interaction radius wide,
whereas extended boxes binned at a corner satisfy neither condition. Reading
``after'' as a box's own footprint, its columns crossed with its rows, is in
consequence incomplete: place one box a column to the right of another and a row
below it, and each footprint runs up and to the right, away from where the other
is binned, so each misses the other by one coordinate and neither ever reads the
other, a loss no rule for deciding which box reports a pair can recover.
Componentwise order on cells is \emph{partial}, and these incomparable pairs are
exactly the ones lost: held to its own footprint the walk drops $3{,}698$ of
$23{,}756$ pairs on one of the test cases and $882$ of $6{,}971$ on a
heavy-tailed size spread.

Row-major linear index $L(c_{0},c_{1}) = c_{1}n_{0} + c_{0}$ is \emph{total},
and over it the walk is complete: for an overlapping pair $j$'s minimum does not
pass $i$'s maximum on either grid axis, so $L(\operatorname{cell}(j.\min)) \le
L(\operatorname{cell}(i.\max))$, the row term settling it when the rows differ
and the column when they agree. Whichever box has the smaller $L$ holds the
other in its range, and equal $L$ means one cell, where the index decides, the
only index test in \cref{alg:cellmin}. Taken literally that range spans whole
rows, and the bounds narrow it: each cell carries the largest upper bound of the
boxes in it, and each row the prefix maximum of those along the row, so one
binary search gives the first column holding anything that reaches back to the
querying box --- left of that box's own column, where an incomparable partner
sits and where a footprint walk never steps --- and skips every column before it
at once. A second bound per cell does the same on the other axis, and
\cref{fig:cellmin} runs the resulting walk through on one scene. That prefix
lets the binning stand with no bound on how many cells a box may span, where a
grid storing a box only at its lower-left cell must cap the box at $k$ cells and
widen every query window by $k$ to match.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The edge-edge query, one pass (count or fill)}
\label{alg:cellmin}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{EdgeToEdgeBroadPhase}{$A$}
  \State grid and cell size as in \cref{alg:cell}; bin each $A_{i}$ into
         $\operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\min)$ alone
  \State $u_{c} \gets \max\{A_{j}.\max_{a_{1}} : j \in c\}$
    \Comment{$-\infty$ where $c$ is empty}
  \State $\mu_{c} \gets \max\{A_{j}.\max_{a_{0}} : j \in c',\; c' \le c \text{ in } \operatorname{row}(c)\}$
    \Comment{running maximum along each row}
  \ForAll{$i$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State $(c_{0}^{b},c_{1}^{b}) \gets \operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\min)$;\quad
           $(c_{0}^{e},c_{1}^{e}) \gets \operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\max)$
    \ForAll{$j$ listed in $(c_{0}^{b},c_{1}^{b})$ \textbf{with} $j > i$}
      \State \Call{Test}{$i$, $j$} \Comment{its own cell, where the index decides}
    \EndFor
    \For{$c_{1} \gets c_{1}^{b}$ \textbf{to} $c_{1}^{e}$}
      \If{$c_{1} = c_{1}^{b}$} $c_{0} \gets c_{0}^{b} + 1$
        \Comment{its own cell is read above}
      \Else{} $c_{0} \gets$ \Call{LowerBound}{$\mu$ on row $c_{1}$ up to $c_{0}^{e}$,
             $A_{i}.\min_{a_{0}}$}
      \EndIf
      \For{$c_{0}$ \textbf{to} $c_{0}^{e}$, cell $c \gets (c_{0},c_{1})$}
        \If{$u_{c} < A_{i}.\min_{a_{1}}$} \textbf{continue}
          \Comment{the cell is not read}
        \EndIf
        \ForAll{$j$ listed in $c$} \Call{Test}{$i$, $j$}
          \Comment{$c$ is ahead of $i$'s cell, so no index test}
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\Function{Test}{$i$, $j$}
  \If{$A_{i}$ and $A_{j}$ are disjoint in 3D, or share a vertex} \Return \EndIf
  \State count, or emit the pair
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Cost follows the same two terms as \cref{alg:cell}, with the binning reduced to
one incidence per box and the query to whatever the bounds leave. Ordering each
cell's entries on the axis the grid does not use, as the grid hybrids
\citet{serpa2020broadmark} evaluates do, would make the scan inside a cell
monotone as well; it culls where cells are densely occupied and pays an ordering
pass everywhere, costing more than it saves over our benchmark, so the cells are
left unordered.
