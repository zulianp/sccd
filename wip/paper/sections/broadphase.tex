\section{Broad phase}
\label{sec:bp}

The broad phase answers the cheaper of the two questions, never evaluating
$\Fdiff$ and never seeing a tolerance: it turns a mesh into candidate pairs,
primitives whose swept boxes overlap and might therefore touch, and hands them to
\cref{sec:np}. It must be conservative, since a discarded pair is never examined
again, and on our benchmark scenes it is the larger share of the host runtime, so
it must be fast. One algorithm over one uniform grid on two of the three axes
serves both its queries, which differ in shape: vertex-face bins a box into every
cell its extent touches (\cref{sec:bp:cell}), while edge-edge, one list against
itself, admits a cheaper binning and a different walk (\cref{sec:bp:self}).

\subsection{Swept boxes, and the shape of the output}
\label{sec:bp:common}

Because vertices move affinely, the axis-aligned box enclosing a primitive's
whole trajectory is the componentwise hull of its vertex positions at $t=0$ and
$t=1$, exact and needing no sampling; one box is built per vertex, per edge and
per face, and where a box is stored in a narrower type than the geometry each
bound moves outward by one unit in the last place, so a rounded box can never
exclude a candidate the exact box would have kept. The overlap test rejects only
on a \emph{strict} separation, $a_{\min} > b_{\max}$ on some axis, so boxes that
merely touch are reported as overlapping --- deliberately, since a candidate
window rejecting boxes in contact drops the degenerate pairs a moving surface
produces most often. All storage is structure-of-arrays and every output is
built \emph{count-then-fill}: a first pass counts the pairs each primitive will
contribute, a prefix sum turns those counts into offsets, and a second pass
re-runs the identical traversal, writing into its own slice, so nothing grows a
shared buffer, the allocation is exact, the output is bit-for-bit reproducible,
and the same shape works on a GPU, where a growable per-thread container is not
available. The host tests one box against $32$ consecutive boxes at a time, an
AVX-512, AVX2 or NEON kernel returning a $32$-bit mask of the survivors, so
counting is a population count and enumeration a bit scan, and a rejected
candidate never issues the indirect load of its element indices, the expensive
part of a test that usually fails.

\subsection{The cell list}
\label{sec:bp:grid}

Both queries read one structure, and it is built once per step. The grid spans
\emph{two} of the three axes: for a surface of $N$ elements of size $h$ in a
region of side $L$ we have $N \sim (L/h)^{2}$, since a surface does not fill a
volume, so a two-dimensional grid at element resolution has $O(N)$ cells and is
mostly occupied, where a three-dimensional one has $(L/h)^{3} = N^{3/2}$ cells
--- at $N = 1.5\times10^{6}$, $1.8\times10^{9}$ cells at $0.08\%$ occupancy,
spending memory and empty-cell iteration to prune along a direction the surface
barely occupies. The third axis still culls, through the ordinary
three-dimensional box test inside the cell, where it costs nothing extra. The
spacing on each of the two axes is the \emph{mean extent of the swept boxes} on
that axis, the resulting cell counts scaled isotropically so that their product
does not exceed $4N$: past that point more resolution does not pay, and a
degenerate extent could otherwise allocate without bound. The caller supplies no
voxel size.

The quantity that sets the spacing is the swept box, not the element it came
from, and the two differ by the distance the element travels in one step. Taking
the \emph{largest} swept box instead of the mean is the textbook uniform-grid
rule, and it buys a property the mean lacks: with no box wider than a cell, two
overlapping boxes lie within one cell of each other, so a fixed $3\times3$
stencil finds every partner with no duplicates to settle. Its cost is that the
widest box in the scene sets the spacing for all of them, and swept box sizes
span a wide range --- the widest is $4.5\times$ the mean on armadillo-rollers'
vertices and $24\times$ on cloth-funnel's edges, since a few elements move fast
while most barely move. On armadillo-rollers one swept edge spans $11\%$ of the
scene, so a cell at least that wide admits an $8\times9$ grid at any element
count, and the scene's $36{,}363$ edges land in $72$ cells, $505$ to a cell,
which measured costs the host broad phase up to $11.9\times$. Splitting the
boxes across a fine grid and a coarse one recovers most of that, and still
loses: with the cut chosen per step by costing every candidate against a
histogram of box sizes, the two-level structure returns identical pair sets and
runs $1.3\times$ to $1.5\times$ behind the single level, because one cut has to
straddle the two to five octaves these scenes span. The mean keeps a box's cost
proportional to its own size, as a heavy-tailed size distribution requires.

Each box enters the \emph{single} cell holding its minimum corner. The cell
array therefore holds exactly one index per box, for both pair types, and its
length is known before it is filled, so construction is the count-then-fill of
\cref{sec:bp:common} with no span count to agree on: the cell a box lands in is
one clamp of one coordinate pair, a pure function of the box and the grid, which
the counting and the scatter cannot read differently. The pair output is built
the same way, so the whole broad phase performs exactly two allocations, both of
counted sizes.

Binning at a corner tells a query nothing about how far the boxes in a cell
reach back towards it, so the structure carries two running maxima, and they are
what make a bounded walk possible (\cref{alg:cell}). Each cell records the
largest upper bound of the boxes in it on the second axis. Along each row, the
cells record the \emph{cumulative} maximum of the largest upper bound on the
first axis, so one binary search over a row gives the first column holding
anything that reaches back to the querying box, and skips every column before it
at once. Those two numbers let the binning stand with no bound on how many cells
a box may span, where a grid storing a box only at its lower-left cell must cap
the box at $k$ cells and widen every query window by $k$ to match.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Building the cell list, shared by both queries}
\label{alg:cell}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{BuildCellList}{$B$}
  \State $(a_{0},a_{1}) \gets$ the two axes of largest centre variance
  \State $h_{k} \gets$ mean extent of the boxes on axis $a_{k}$;\quad
         $n_{k} \gets \lfloor \operatorname{span}_{k}/h_{k} \rfloor$,
         scaled so $n_{0}n_{1} \le 4N$
  \State $\operatorname{cell}(i) \gets L\bigl(\operatorname{clamp}_{0}(B_{i}.\min_{a_{0}}),
         \operatorname{clamp}_{1}(B_{i}.\min_{a_{1}})\bigr)$
         \Comment{one cell per box, both passes}
  \State count $\operatorname{cell}(i)$ over all $i$, prefix-sum into \textit{cellptr},
         scatter each $i$ into $\operatorname{cellidx}$
  \ForAll{cells $c$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State $\mathit{hi}_{1}[c] \gets \max_{i \in c} B_{i}.\max_{a_{1}}$
      \Comment{how far the cell reaches on the second axis}
    \State $\mathit{pre}[c] \gets \max_{i \in c} B_{i}.\max_{a_{0}}$
  \EndFor
  \ForAll{rows $r$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State $\mathit{pre}[r,c] \gets \max_{c' \le c} \mathit{pre}[r,c']$
      \Comment{cumulative along the row}
  \EndFor
  \State $k \gets \max_{i} \bigl(\operatorname{clamp}_{1}(B_{i}.\max_{a_{1}})
         - \operatorname{clamp}_{1}(B_{i}.\min_{a_{1}})\bigr)$
         \Comment{rows a box may span; read by the vertex-face walk}
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

What the maxima cannot do is license a walk over a box's own footprint.
Componentwise order on cells is \emph{partial}: place one box a column to the
right of another and a row below it, and each footprint runs up and to the right,
away from where the other is binned, so each misses the other by one coordinate
and neither ever reads the other --- a loss no rule for deciding which box
reports a pair can recover. Held to its own footprint such a walk drops
$3{,}698$ of $23{,}756$ pairs on one of the test cases and $882$ of $6{,}971$ on
a heavy-tailed size spread. Row-major linear index $L(c_{0},c_{1}) = c_{1}n_{0} +
c_{0}$ is \emph{total}, and over it a walk is complete: for an overlapping pair
$j$'s minimum does not pass $i$'s maximum on either grid axis, so
$L(\operatorname{cell}(j.\min)) \le L(\operatorname{cell}(i.\max))$, the row term
settling it when the rows differ and the column when they agree. The two queries
below differ in how much of that range each must read.

Nothing is sorted and nothing assumes an order within a cell, since a query
tests every entry of a cell it reads and never breaks on a first miss. Binning
costs $O(N)$ and a query $O\bigl(\sum_{i}\sum_{c}\text{occupancy}(c)\bigr)$ over
the cells $c$ it reads, with no logarithmic factor beyond the one binary search
per row. \Cref{sec:results:scaling} measures the exponents both achieve in
practice.

\subsection{The vertex-face query}
\label{sec:bp:cell}

Vertex-face takes two lists, and the faces are the list that is binned: a vertex
then walks the cells that can hold a face reaching it. Above and to the right the
range needs nothing stored, since a face overlapping a vertex has its minimum
corner at or below the vertex's maximum on both axes, so no cell past the
vertex's own maximum cell is ever read. Below and to the left the two maxima of
\cref{sec:bp:grid} bound it: per row, the cumulative maximum gives the first
column holding a face that reaches back, by the one binary search; across rows,
the largest number of rows any face spans bounds how far below the vertex's own
row a reaching face can be binned. Because a face occupies one cell, a pair is
met exactly once and no duplicate rule is needed.

Binning the faces rather than the vertices is what makes that cheap. The cells
are sized from the list that is binned, so binning the vertices gives
vertex-sized cells that a face --- much larger than a vertex's own sweep ---
must walk a rectangle of, paying a duplicate test on every surviving candidate.
Indexing the faces at their minimum corner costs one entry each instead,
measured at a third to a seventh of the cells read per emitted pair, and the pair
set is unchanged: only where a pair is found differs.

\subsection{The edge-edge query}
\label{sec:bp:self}

Edge-edge is one list against itself, so each box walks \emph{forward} from its
own cell under the total order of \cref{sec:bp:grid}, and the whole range above
its own maximum cell goes unread because a partner there is the one that reads
it. Reading only the cells that come after a box's own is how linked-cell
neighbour search avoids handling a pair twice, each cell visiting half its
neighbours~\citep{allen2017liquids}; that walk is sound there because a particle
is a point binned by its centre in a cell at least the interaction radius wide,
whereas extended boxes binned at a corner satisfy neither condition, which is
what the total order repairs. Equal $L$ means one cell, where the index decides
which of the two boxes reports the pair, and that is the only index test in
\cref{alg:cellmin}. The row the box is binned in needs no search, since the
walk starts from the box's own column.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The edge-edge query, one pass (count or fill)}
\label{alg:cellmin}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{EdgeToEdgeBroadPhase}{$A$}
  \State grid and cell size as in \cref{alg:cell}; bin each $A_{i}$ into
         $\operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\min)$ alone
  \State $u_{c} \gets \max\{A_{j}.\max_{a_{1}} : j \in c\}$
    \Comment{$-\infty$ where $c$ is empty}
  \State $\mu_{c} \gets \max\{A_{j}.\max_{a_{0}} : j \in c',\; c' \le c \text{ in } \operatorname{row}(c)\}$
    \Comment{running maximum along each row}
  \ForAll{$i$ \textbf{in parallel}}
    \State $(c_{0}^{b},c_{1}^{b}) \gets \operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\min)$;\quad
           $(c_{0}^{e},c_{1}^{e}) \gets \operatorname{cell}(A_{i}.\max)$
    \ForAll{$j$ listed in $(c_{0}^{b},c_{1}^{b})$ \textbf{with} $j > i$}
      \State \Call{Test}{$i$, $j$} \Comment{its own cell, where the index decides}
    \EndFor
    \For{$c_{1} \gets c_{1}^{b}$ \textbf{to} $c_{1}^{e}$}
      \If{$c_{1} = c_{1}^{b}$} $c_{0} \gets c_{0}^{b} + 1$
        \Comment{its own cell is read above}
      \Else{} $c_{0} \gets$ \Call{LowerBound}{$\mu$ on row $c_{1}$ up to $c_{0}^{e}$,
             $A_{i}.\min_{a_{0}}$}
      \EndIf
      \For{$c_{0}$ \textbf{to} $c_{0}^{e}$, cell $c \gets (c_{0},c_{1})$}
        \If{$u_{c} < A_{i}.\min_{a_{1}}$} \textbf{continue}
          \Comment{the cell is not read}
        \EndIf
        \ForAll{$j$ listed in $c$} \Call{Test}{$i$, $j$}
          \Comment{$c$ is ahead of $i$'s cell, so no index test}
        \EndFor
      \EndFor
    \EndFor
  \EndFor
\EndFunction
\Function{Test}{$i$, $j$}
  \If{$A_{i}$ and $A_{j}$ are disjoint in 3D, or share a vertex} \Return \EndIf
  \State count, or emit the pair
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Intra-cell ordering was measured and rejected: ordering each cell by its second
coordinate lets a walk break out of a cell early, and over the benchmark it
loses to the unordered walk on every scene, because the order has to be rebuilt
every step while the walk it saves is already bounded by the two maxima.
